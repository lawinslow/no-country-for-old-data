The Gift of Non-Opposition: A Love Letter to Boston's Public Input Process

April 2026 · Boston Housing

Boston has built something truly special.

Most cities, when they want to know what residents think about a proposed development, conduct a survey, hold an informational session, maybe post a comment form online. Boston has done something more ambitious. Boston has constructed an elaborate civic ritual — the Public Input Session — whose function, after decades of refinement, is now almost perfectly optimized for a single output: opposition.

It did not go smoothly, reported the Jamaica Plain Bulletin, of a recent meeting about a 35-unit apartment building on Washington Street. Almost total opposition.

Almost total. Someone, somewhere, was not opposed. We don't know who. They have not been found.

"Few people on the call agreed with that," the article noted, of a point made in favor of the project.

Few. Not zero. Somewhere out there, a brave few. Huddled together. Agreeing, quietly, that maybe people need places to live.

This is democracy.

I renovated my basement last year.

To do this — to modify the interior of my own home, the home I own, the home I pay taxes on, the home whose basement has always been my basement — I was required to apply for a zoning variance. This is normal. I understood this. I prepared my application, attended my hearing, and waited.

What I did not expect was the neighbors.

They came to me, one by one and sometimes in pairs, and they said: we'll support this.

They said it the way someone says I'll put in a good word for you. They said it the way a king grants a petition. They said it like they were doing something — like their non-intervention in my basement was a gift they had chosen to give me, having weighed the alternatives, having considered whether to oppose the insulation I planned to install, having decided, in their generosity, to let me have it.

I thanked them. I thanked them for their support of my basement. I am still not sure what I did wrong that I found this normal.

Here is what Boston's input process has accomplished, over many years of careful cultivation:

It has taught everyone that opposition is the default. That the correct response to any proposed change — a building, a bike lane, a tree removal, a basement renovation — is to first decide whether to oppose it, and then, if you choose not to, to make sure the person knows you chose not to. Your neutrality is a favor. Your non-interference is a contribution. You have given something by doing nothing, and you would like credit.

Meanwhile, the person who needs the housing, or the variance, or the permit, must come before a room — or a Zoom call — full of people whose entire civic identity has been formed around the right to object. They must make their case. They must take questions. They must listen while a man asks where the construction vehicles are supposed to go. They must hear safety, safety, safety.

They almost certainly will not prevail.

Other cities have started asking a different question. Instead of who opposes this?, they ask: is there a reason to prohibit it? Instead of making the applicant justify the project to the neighbors, they make the opponents justify their opposition to the city. This is called, in planning circles, "by-right permitting," and in Boston circles, it is called "not how we do things."

We have a process. The process produces opposition. The opposition produces delay. The delay produces nothing, or occasionally a smaller building with more parking, after which everyone goes home and rents remain high and the man with the funeral home still owns the four parcels he bought to prevent this from happening.

This is the system working. This is what working looks like.

Somewhere tonight there is a homeowner filling out a variance application. Maybe they want to add a dormer. Maybe they want to enclose a porch. Maybe they just want to finish the basement, install some drywall, make it livable.

Their neighbors will gather. The neighbors will deliberate. Some will oppose. Some, in their great generosity, will support.

The homeowner will thank them. They will mean it. After enough of this, you start to mean it.

Boston has built a system where strangers decide what you do with your basement, and then you thank them for letting you.

Almost total opposition. Few people agreed.

This is the city that is going to figure out the housing crisis.

I believe in us.

The author applied for a zoning variance to renovate their own home and was told by their neighbors that they had decided to support it. The author said thank you. The author is still thinking about this.
