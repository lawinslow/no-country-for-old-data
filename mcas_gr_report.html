Massachusetts Graduation Rates & MCAS Scores

Analysis prepared March 2026. Data: DESE / E2C Hub / MCAS Service Center.

October 2026 update: Grade 10 MCAS results for 2026 are now available. See Section 7. Statewide grade 10 scores fell again, and grade 10 ELA % Meeting or Exceeding is down 12 points since 2024. Class of 2026 graduation rates have not been released yet. Section 4 was also revised to compare graduation rates with grade 10 scores instead of grades 3–8.

Key finding: Massachusetts graduation rates hit an all-time high in 2025 (Class of 2025), coinciding with the November 2024 passage of Ballot Question 2, which eliminated the MCAS test as a hard graduation requirement. At the same time, grade 10 MCAS scores for the same cohort were at post-plateau lows — the starkest decoupling of scores and graduation rates in the dataset.

4. Did Ballot Question 2 Raise Graduation Rates?

Revised October 2026. The original version compared graduation rates with grades 3–8 MCAS results, which were roughly flat in 2025. This version uses grade 10, the test that was the graduation requirement. Graduation rates are from the E2C Hub dataset n2xa-p822 (4-year rate, all students), which matches the March scrape.

Ballot Question 2 passed November 5, 2024 (59% Yes), eliminating MCAS as a graduation requirement effective December 5, 2024 — mid-way through the Class of 2025's senior year. Students who had not yet passed the MCAS were immediately eligible to graduate through the new coursework-based pathway.

Evidence for a policy effect

  • Statewide mean grad rate rose +1.16 pp, the largest single-year gain since 2006–2007, and statistically significant (t=4.57, p<0.0001).
  • The gain was concentrated in lower-performing districts — exactly where the MCAS requirement would have been most binding.
  • Grade 10 MCAS fell at the same time. Across 298 districts with both measures, grade 10 % Meeting or Exceeding dropped an average of 7.2 points in ELA and 4.7 in math from 2024 to 2025. ELA fell in 80% of districts. Rising graduation rates can't be explained by better academic results.
  • Of the 68 districts whose graduation rate rose 3 points or more, 58 saw grade 10 ELA decline, 2 were flat and 8 improved.

5. MCAS Scores Over Time — Calibrated Long-Run Series

Why calibration is needed

Legacy MCAS (through 2018) uses a 200–280 scaled score and CPI (Composite Performance Index, 0–100). Next Gen MCAS (2019+) uses a 440–560 scaled score. No official crosswalk exists; DESE explicitly warns against comparing the two series directly.

Calibration method

Using the 2018 legacy CPI and 2019 Next Gen average scaled score for 297 matched districts, we fit a linear regression for ELA and Math separately. The 2018→2019 handoff is seamless: calibrated 2018 ELA predicts 505.6, actual 2019 was 506.0; Math predicts 503.9, actual 505.0.

The ELA R² of 0.47 reflects meaningful noise. Calibrated ELA values are directionally reliable but should be treated as approximate. The Math fit (R²=0.69) is stronger and more credible.

MCAS scores and graduation rates moved broadly together through ~2015. After that they decoupled: scores plateaued then declined, while graduation rates plateaued near 89–90%. In 2025, the cohort with post-plateau low MCAS scores achieved the highest graduation rate on record — consistent with the removal of the test-based graduation gate.

Cross-sectional correlation between MCAS meet/exceed % and district grad rates remains meaningful within each year (r≈0.60), suggesting MCAS still tracks underlying academic preparation. But year-over-year ΔMCAS and Δgrad rate are essentially uncorrelated (r≈0.02), meaning short-term MCAS changes don't predict short-term graduation rate changes.

Takeaways:
  • Grade 10 ELA % Meeting or Exceeding fell from 57% (2024) to 45% (2026), the lowest in the Next Gen series. Math fell from 48% to 44%, and science from 49% to 41%.
  • The decline is broad. Grade 10 scores dropped in most districts in every subject, while grade 8 was mixed.
  • Participation stayed around 97%. Students are still taking the test; they are scoring lower.

Which schools declined? (school-level, average scaled score)

To see whether the decline was concentrated in lower-performing schools, this compares 326 high schools (ELA) and 324 (math) with at least 30 students tested in every year from 2022 to 2026. It uses average scaled score rather than % Meeting or Exceeding, because a percentage based on one cutoff exaggerates changes at schools with many students near that cutoff. Schools are grouped by their 2022 score. The changes measured (2023→24 before Ballot Question 2, and 2024→26 after) don't include 2022, so a lucky or unlucky 2022 can't create a false pattern.

Findings:
  • The decline was not concentrated in lower-performing schools. Every group of schools fell. ELA fell by similar amounts in every performance group (5.4 to 8.0 points over two years), with the middle group falling slightly more. In math, the lowest- and highest-scoring schools fell least (−2.8 and −2.3), and the middle fell most (−5.0).
  • The share of low-income students made no meaningful difference. Schools with the most low-income students fell 5.7 points in ELA, and schools with the fewest fell 5.8. Compared with each group's own pre-2024 trend, schools with more low-income students actually broke less sharply, because the more affluent schools had been improving before 2024.
  • Within the same schools, low-income students and other students declined almost equally: −7.3 vs. −6.9 in ELA and −4.1 vs. −4.3 in math, across 236 schools with at least 20 students in each group. Statewide, both groups fell 7 points in ELA.
Interpretation: Before 2025, every student needed to pass grade 10 MCAS, but the students at real risk of failing were concentrated in lower-performing schools. If losing that risk were the whole story, scores would have dropped most there. Instead, the decline is broad and nearly uniform. That fits a general loss of stakes for everyone, for example less effort from students who would have passed easily, and less test preparation and attention from schools. A uniform shift is also what a change in the test itself would look like, so this result makes that explanation more important to rule out. Grade 8, which had no such stakes, did not decline.
Interpretation: These results fit lower student effort on a test that no longer counts toward a diploma. They do not prove it. This analysis has not ruled out other explanations, such as changes to the grade 10 tests themselves or differences between classes beyond what the grade 8 comparison controls for.

Note: Section 4 was revised alongside this update. Its original "essentially zero" MCAS change for 2024→2025 came from grades 3–8 results. Grade 10, the test that was the graduation requirement, fell sharply that year.

8. Background: MCAS as a Graduation Requirement

  • 2003: MCAS first required for graduation. Minimum score: 220 (Needs Improvement).
  • 2010: Threshold raised to 240 (Proficient). EPP pathway still available at 220.
  • 2019: Transition to Next Gen MCAS for grade 10. Interim cut scores set via matched-sample analysis.
  • 2022: COVID waiver — Class of 2022 could substitute relevant coursework. Effect on grad rates was modest (+0.2 pp mean, Q1 +1.8 pp).
  • August 2022: Board voted to raise the bar for Classes of 2026–2031 to 486/500 — never implemented.
  • November 5, 2024: Ballot Question 2 passed (59% Yes). Funded primarily by the Massachusetts Teachers Association ($16M+).
  • December 5, 2024: MCAS graduation requirement eliminated. Students mid-year in the Class of 2025 immediately eligible under new coursework pathway.
  • Class of 2026+: Graduation requires: 2 years HS ELA, Algebra I + Geometry, 1 year science. MCAS continues statewide but scores no longer gate diploma issuance.
