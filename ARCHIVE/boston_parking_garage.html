In Defense of a Parking Garage That Has Been Empty Since 1928

April 2026 · Boston Housing

Some things are worth protecting.

A parking garage at 406 South Huntington Avenue in Jamaica Plain was built in 1928. It has, for reasons that are not entirely clear but that we must assume are good ones, been vacant for some time. It sits at a corner lot spanning 11,000 square feet, steps from a Whole Foods, a CVS, a 7-Eleven, a Boston Public Library branch, an Ethiopian restaurant, a cannabis retailer, and the 39 bus. It is surrounded, in other words, by every amenity that urban planners spend their careers trying to put within walking distance of housing.

The parking garage is empty.

Developer Frank Schillace wants to build 48 apartments there. Five stories. Eight affordable units. One-bedroom and two-bedroom homes for 48 families, at one of the most transit-connected corners in Jamaica Plain, on land that is currently a vacant building that has not functioned as a parking garage in living memory.

Bernie Doherty and Alcurtis Clark believe it should be four stories.

I want to talk about that fourth floor.

What is a floor? It is, in physical terms, approximately ten to twelve feet of vertical space. It contains, in a 48-unit building, perhaps eight to ten apartments. These apartments might house twelve to fifteen people. Twelve to fifteen people who would live near the Whole Foods, near the library, near the 39 bus, near the Ethiopian restaurant — people who would walk to those places, spend money at those places, use the library, ride the bus, exist in the neighborhood in the way that residents of neighborhoods do.

Bernie Doherty and Alcurtis Clark looked at that floor — at those ten apartments, those fifteen people — and said: no. Four stories. Not five.

This is the correct instinct. I want to explain why.

A four-story building is a building you can look at and feel comfortable with. A five-story building is a building that is one story taller than a four-story building. Once you allow five stories, the next developer will ask for six. Then seven. Then, as has apparently already happened in Hyde Park, someone will be asking the fire department if their ladders go that high. The answer, we have learned, is yes. This has been described by a community leader as a "bummer." It is a bummer. Bernie Doherty and Alcurtis Clark are trying to prevent more bummers.

The fourth floor must not be built. The fifteen people who would have lived there will simply have to find somewhere else, in a metro area where the average one-bedroom costs over $3,000 a month, which is a problem that has nothing to do with this parking garage.

The attorney, Richard Lynds, noted that he hears the same objections citywide. He said this as though it were notable. As though hearing the same objections, in meeting after meeting, in neighborhood after neighborhood, about project after project, were evidence of something other than the objections being correct.

The objections are the same everywhere because the problem is everywhere. The problem is buildings that are one story taller than they should be. The solution is the same everywhere too: four stories. Not five. We do not need to have a different conversation in each neighborhood. The conversation is the same. The answer is four stories. The parking garage can stay.

The site is next to the JP Laundromat Center.

I mention this because I think it is important context. The JP Laundromat Center has been there for years, presumably. It has coexisted peacefully with the vacant parking garage. The 7-Eleven has been there. The CVS has been there. The cannabis retailer has been there. And the parking garage, empty, built in 1928, has sat among them, not housing anyone, not doing anything, simply existing as a reminder that not every corner needs to be developed, not every vacancy needs to be filled, not every piece of land zoned for housing needs to actually contain housing.

This is called character. You either feel it or you don't.

Forty-eight families cannot give a corner character. A vacant 1928 parking garage can. This is the thing that is difficult to explain to people who haven't been to enough community meetings, who haven't sat in enough folding chairs, who haven't felt in their bones the difference between four stories and five.

Kathy Brown said it "fits in pretty well," the five stories. She supported the height.

Kathy Brown has not been to enough meetings.

The project is at "a very early stage," the attorney said. Good. At the early stage, we can still fix this. We can bring it back to four stories. We can preserve the relationship between this neighborhood and the concept of four stories, which is a relationship worth preserving, which is a number worth defending, which is — I want to be precise here — one less than five.

The parking garage has been empty since 1928. It has waited this long.

It can wait a little longer.

The author prefers four stories to five and is available to explain this at any community meeting, at any time, for any project, in any neighborhood. The objections are the same everywhere because they are correct everywhere.
