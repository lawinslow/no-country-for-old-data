Boston Knows What It's Doing

April 2026 · Boston Housing

We live in complicated times.

The global economy is changing faster than anyone can track. AI is reshaping entire industries. Climate change is restructuring where and how people live. Cities across the world are in an arms race for talent, for workers, for the young and educated professionals who will build the next century's economy. Boston — with its universities, its hospitals, its biotech corridors — is uniquely positioned to win that race.

But winning requires clarity. It requires knowing, when everything is chaos and the future is uncertain, what you are actually for.

Boston knows. Boston has always known.

The dead.

At a recent community meeting in Forest Hills, residents gathered to discuss a proposed six-story, 35-unit apartment building on Washington Street. The developer had done everything right: affordable units, transit-adjacent location, modest footprint. The kind of project that housing economists, urban planners, and every serious study of the Boston metro area says we desperately need more of.

And then Michael West, owner of Brady and Fallon Funeral Home, stood up and asked: where are the construction vehicles supposed to go?

The room understood immediately. Here was a man who had seen past the noise — past the think-pieces about affordability, past the hand-wringing about young people leaving the city, past the data and the charts and the economists — to the thing that actually matters. The funeral home. The 300 annual funerals. The parking.

While other cities are distracted by the living — their commutes, their rents, their childcare costs, their career prospects — Boston is playing a longer game. The longest game, you might say.

Singapore is building vertical cities. Tokyo is reforming its zoning to allow density almost everywhere. Auckland eliminated single-family zoning citywide. These cities look at their housing crises and see an economic emergency requiring urgent structural reform.

Boston looks at its housing crisis and sees an opportunity to protect funeral home access lanes.

Who's really thinking long-term here?

The critics will say: Boston is one of the most expensive rental markets in America. That the average one-bedroom apartment costs over $3,000 a month. That the region turns away talent, hemorrhages young workers, prices out the nurses and teachers and bus drivers without whom the city does not function.

And to that I say: there is a dangerous intersection on Washington Street. Rebecca Keane said "safety" three times. You don't say something three times unless you mean it three times. That's just math.

Masua Dibingo is 100 percent opposed. Not to housing in general — she's very clear about that — but to these 35 units, at this location, near this funeral home, in this economy. When you add it all up, the math is simple: the living can figure something out. The dead have nowhere else to go.

Boston's great insight — the thing that separates it from lesser cities stumbling toward the future — is its understanding that a city is not just for the people who live in it today, or who might live in it tomorrow. A city is for everyone who has ever lived in it. Their memory. Their rest. The quiet dignity of their final parking arrangements.

In a volatile world, that is a kind of stability. That is a vision.

Build housing somewhere. Build it anywhere. Build it in whatever city doesn't have the wisdom to understand what Boston understands.

We have funerals to think about.

This post was inspired by reporting in the Jamaica Plain Bulletin on community opposition to a proposed apartment building at 3716 Washington Street in Forest Hills.
