The Saturday That Didn’t Happen

October 2026 · Data: GTFS-realtime vehicle positions collected continuously since 29 August 2026

Summary: Buses leaving Roslindale Square on a Saturday morning lose about a minute and a quarter to traffic in an 800-metre stretch north of the Square — roughly three times the weekday figure for the same hours, in a window when no bus lane is in force. On 26 September a nor’easter hit Boston and that congestion vanished entirely: not reduced, zero. The same number of buses drove the same road. Across the rest of the MBTA bus network that storm changed Saturday congestion by six percent. This is a natural experiment that costs nothing to run, and it points at a 4½-hour window the corridor’s bus lanes do not cover.

1. A corridor that is worse on Saturday than on a weekday

Washington Street in Roslindale has bus lanes inbound in the morning and outbound in the afternoon. Neither operates at the weekend. For most of the week that hardly matters — Saturday and Sunday are quieter than weekdays almost everywhere on the MBTA.

Not here. Measuring the time an outbound bus spends halted — standing still more than 35 metres from any stop, so stuck in traffic rather than picking anybody up — Saturday late mornings on this corridor are worse than any weekday hour outside the evening peak. Between 10am and 2pm the average outbound bus loses 79 seconds. The weekday average for the same hours is 24.

2. It is one specific 800 metres

The excess is not spread along the corridor. It sits between roughly 200 and 1,000 metres north of the Poplar Street intersection — from Roslindale Square up past Firth Road and Granfield Avenue to about Whipple Avenue. South of the Square there is nothing. Past Whipple there is nothing.

3. Then it rained, and the whole thing disappeared

On Saturday 26 September a nor’easter crossed eastern Massachusetts. Blue Hill Observatory recorded 1.29 inches of rain by 1pm and a high of 58°F, eleven degrees below normal. The farmers market, which runs rain or shine, went ahead.

Three Saturdays agree closely: 89, 74 and 76 seconds, with the median bus losing more than a minute and 80 to 93 per cent of buses stopping at least once. On 26 September the mean was 9 seconds, the median was zero, and in the critical 800-metre band the accumulated halt time in every single 200-metre bin was exactly 0.0 seconds.

It is not a detour and it is not a data gap. The number of trips through each 200-metre bin is the same on all four Saturdays — 51 to 53 — across all nine routes. What changes is the number of position reports those buses emit while crossing the band: 1,360, 1,002 and 1,147 on the market Saturdays against 399 on the storm Saturday. A bus that is stopped keeps reporting from the same spot. Three times fewer reports over the same ground, from the same number of buses, is what free flow looks like.

4. The control: the storm did almost nothing to the rest of the network

Rain slows traffic everywhere, so on its own a quiet storm Saturday proves little. The same measurement was therefore run across every MBTA bus route for the same four Saturdays and the same hours, using identical code.

Six per cent. The storm that erased this corridor’s Saturday congestion was, for the MBTA bus network as a whole, an ordinary Saturday. Whatever is happening on Washington Street is local and intensely weather-sensitive — the signature of discretionary trips, not of through traffic.

5. What this does and does not establish

It establishes that the Saturday congestion is demand rather than a permanent feature of the street. The road did not change on 26 September; the number of buses did not change; what changed is that fewer people chose to drive to Roslindale Square. Remove the discretionary trips and 800 metres of corridor goes to free flow.

The obvious candidate for that demand is the farmers market, which runs at Adams Park beside the Square from 9:00 to 1:30 on exactly these Saturdays, and whose window brackets the congestion almost precisely. That part remains a hypothesis. These data cannot separate the market from everything else a dry Saturday morning brings to a village centre, and a market that runs rain or shine still ran on the storm day — it is attendance that collapsed, not the market. Confirming it would need an observer, or a Saturday when the market is cancelled for some reason other than weather.

Caveats. Four Saturdays, one of them the storm. Halt detection treats a bus as stopped when consecutive position reports show it moving under 2 km/h; anything within 35 metres of a stop is counted as dwell and excluded, so a bus held at a red light beside a stop is not counted. The corridor measurement covers routes 30, 34, 34E, 35, 36, 37, 40, 50 and 51 between Metropolitan Avenue and the Forest Hills approach, with the terminal itself excluded.

6. Why it matters for the lanes

The inbound lane runs 5–10am. The outbound lane runs 2–7pm. Neither operates at weekends, and the Saturday peak lands at 11am — in the gap between the two windows even on a weekday. The stretch that goes to zero in a storm is the same ground the weekday afternoon outbound lane already covers; on Saturday that curb is open to general traffic and parking.

So this is the corridor running a controlled experiment on itself: same street, same buses, same geometry, protection removed, three times the delay — and a storm week showing what the street does when the demand is taken away. Any argument about what a bus lane is worth here no longer has to be made by comparison with Columbus Avenue or with the rest of the network. It can be made from this corridor alone.
