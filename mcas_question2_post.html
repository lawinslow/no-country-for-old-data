Question 2 Gave Us Lower 10th Grade Scores. Nobody Knows How Bad It Is.

October 2026 · Data: DESE MCAS results, 2019–2026

Summary: Two years after Massachusetts stopped requiring students to pass MCAS to graduate, 10th grade English scores have fallen 12 points, to the lowest level since this version of the test began. Eighth grade scores didn't fall. The state's explanation, that weaker students are coming up from middle school, doesn't fit its own data. The decline is also not concentrated in struggling schools. It's nearly the same everywhere. Either 10th graders are learning much less, or the test has stopped measuring what they know. We can't currently tell which, and that was supposed to be the one thing we kept.

The number

In spring 2026, 45% of Massachusetts 10th graders met or exceeded expectations in English. Two years ago it was 57%. In 2019 it was 61%.

For a sense of scale: COVID knocked grades 3–8 English down 6 points (52% to 46%), and that drop got years of headlines, federal relief money and tutoring programs. This is twice that, in two years, with no pandemic.

The reaction has been muted. That's partly because everyone thinks they already know the reason.

The promise

Question 2 passed in November 2024 with 59% of the vote. It ended the requirement that students pass the 10th grade MCAS to get a diploma. The central reassurance of the campaign was that the test itself wasn't going anywhere. The Massachusetts Teachers Association told members that MCAS would still be given, "but used for diagnostic purposes as originally intended." Matt Damon put it more simply in the campaign's ad: "We'd keep the MCAS and use it for what it is intended for."

That was a reasonable promise to make. We did keep the test. Nearly every 10th grader still takes it, with participation around 97% every year. What's unclear is whether it still tells us anything.

The official explanation doesn't fit

When this year's results came out, DESE's Rob Curtin pushed back on the idea that students had stopped trying. "I wouldn't say that it was necessarily that they weren't trying," he told WBUR. His explanation was that fewer students are meeting standards by the end of 8th grade, so it makes sense they'd struggle in high school.

That's checkable with the state's own public data. Each 10th grade class took MCAS as 8th graders two years earlier. If weaker 8th graders explained weaker 10th graders, the classes that dropped should have looked weaker in 8th grade.

They didn't. This year's 10th graders scored 43% as 8th graders, slightly better than the class that reached 57% in 10th grade. What changed is what happened between 8th and 10th grade. The classes before Question 2 gained 15 to 17 points between those tests. The two classes since have gained 7 and 2.

This is a statewide comparison of percentages, not individual students, and the two tests measure different grade-level standards. But the pattern is hard to square with a weaker pipeline.

It isn't where you'd expect

The simplest version of the "no more stakes" story goes like this: students who were at risk of failing used to get extra help and extra pressure. That help and pressure went away, so their scores dropped. If that were the whole story, the decline would be concentrated in the lower-performing schools, where those students were.

It isn't. I grouped about 325 high schools by how they scored in 2022 and compared how much each group fell from 2024 to 2026, using average scores rather than percentages (details in the full analysis):

The top schools fell about as much as the bottom ones. Grouping schools by their share of low-income students shows the same thing. Within the same schools, low-income students and other students fell by nearly identical amounts (−7.3 and −6.9 points). Whatever happened, it happened to almost everyone.

The fork

There are two ways to read a broad, sudden decline in one grade's scores.

Students know less. High schools changed what they teach once the test stopped mattering, and 10th graders are genuinely less prepared in reading and writing. If so, this is a serious problem, and the fact that it showed up across every type of school would make it more serious, not less.

The test stopped measuring what students know. When a test has no consequences, many students put in less effort, especially on long written answers. Their scores then understate what they can do. If so, students may be fine, but Massachusetts no longer has a reliable measure of 10th grade achievement.

Most of the evidence points toward the second:

  • Grade 8 didn't drop. The same students, tested two years earlier with no change in stakes, held steady.
  • English fell much more than math (12 points vs. 4). The English test includes long essays, which is where low effort shows up most.
  • DESE saw it directly in 2025. After the first post-Question 2 test, Curtin pointed to an increase in blank or off-topic answers and said there was "some evidence" of a motivation effect. A year later, the explanation changed.
  • The drop is uniform. A loss of effort across the whole grade fits a broad decline better than a change in instruction would.

But none of this is proof. And it doesn't have to be one or the other. Schools that no longer have to get every student over the bar may also be spending less time on what the test covers.

The fair counterargument: Jack Schneider of UMass Amherst called the results "fairly typical year to year fluctuations" and said we shouldn't conclude that students are learning less. Supporters of Question 2 could reasonably add that less test prep was the point. That may be right. But if scores now move for reasons unrelated to learning, then the test can't do the diagnostic job we were promised it would keep doing.

How to settle it

DESE has the data to answer this, and most of it could be analyzed in weeks:

  • Publish the blank and off-topic rates for 2024, 2025 and 2026. DESE cited them in 2025. They're the most direct measure of effort available.
  • Compare essay scores with multiple-choice scores. If essays collapsed and multiple choice held, that's effort, not knowledge.
  • Look at time spent on the test. The test is taken on computers, so DESE can see students who raced through it.
  • Follow these classes to the SAT. The Class of 2027, the first to take 10th grade MCAS after Question 2, took the SAT as juniors last spring. If their SAT scores held up, the MCAS drop was mostly effort. DESE's SAT data for that year should be public around early 2027.

Why it matters now

The 10th grade test still counts toward school accountability ratings, so high schools may now be judged partly on how motivated their students felt on test day. A statewide council's proposal for new graduation requirements, which Commissioner Martinez has pointed to, includes end-of-course exams. The MTA opposes them. Business groups have cited these results to argue for them. Both sides are now arguing over a number that may not mean what it used to.

The ballot question was about whether one test should decide who graduates. Voters answered that. They weren't asked whether the state should stop knowing how its high school students are doing, and the promise was that it wouldn't. Right now, it doesn't.
